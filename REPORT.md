# Development report

What was built, day by day, in plain words. Newest first.
One paragraph per day — enough that someone who has never seen the code knows what changed and why.

---

## 22 September 2026

The user came back to the platform, found most of their nine firms saying research had failed after a long run, nothing at all in Actual leads, and asked what on earth was happening. Three separate things were going on, and only the first was our doing. On Friday night the engine was restarted several times while the email screen was being built, and each restart killed whatever research was running underneath it; seven runs died about halfway through their twenty-seven steps. The engine recorded exactly that, in plain words, on each killed run -- but the screen never showed the reason, and worse, it announced "research finished with a failed step" when in truth no step had failed at all, the run had simply been cut off. So the user was sent hunting for a broken step that did not exist. The screen now says a run stopped, how far it got, and prints the engine's own reason underneath, and the five firms whose websites work were researched again today, each finishing cleanly in about two minutes. The second thing is the missing address-checking credits: every lead must have a checked address before it can be handed to anyone, so with that switched off the best any lead can be is one step away, which is exactly what the screen shows. The third is the firms themselves. Two of the nine have websites that cannot be read at all -- one domain does not exist any more and the other's security certificate is broken -- and the engine deliberately keeps retrying rather than writing them off, so they sit quietly saying "not crawled yet" with nothing on screen to explain it. Four more were read in full, twice by the cheaper reader and once by the expensive one, and not one of those pages ever says how the firm charges its clients; the engine is forbidden to guess that, so those firms stay untiered and cannot be called a fit. One is a patent firm and was rejected as off-profile, and the single genuine lead of the batch, a personal injury firm with twenty-eight people and their addresses on file, is complete but for the address check. None of that was visible on screen, which is the real complaint: the work is honest but silent, and a stuck lead should say what it is stuck on. Later the same day the user said plainly that the enrichment they had asked for was not there: no business profile read, no reviews read, no addresses checked. That was fair. Two of those three wait on credits that have not arrived, and the third, the analysis that decides what a firm actually needs, did not need them at all, so it was built. The engine now reads everything it holds on a firm -- what the site says and lacks, the people, the testimonials, the reviews, the listing, the open roles, the news, and what its own rules already flagged -- and asks the AI what hurts. The rule is that it must prove every answer: each point has to quote one line of that record, and anything it cannot quote is thrown away and the throw-away written down. On the user's own firms it found a site whose copyright stopped in 2013, a firm with seventeen attorneys and nobody listed to support them, and firms with no way for a new client to get in touch; on three others it reported honestly that the record proves nothing, which is the answer that keeps the rest trustworthy. Three more points it offered were dropped for want of proof. It costs about a penny a firm, it runs when a firm is researched rather than every night, and it shows on the lead and on the firm as "What hurts", with each line quoted, its source named, and what it costs the firm spelled out. A specialist review of the wording and the checking was run before any of it went live, and everything it found that held up was fixed -- including two ways a made-up answer could have slipped through the check, and one way a true answer was being thrown away. Last, with a walk-through for the team coming, the home page was made to say plainly what is built and what is not. The engine now keeps that map itself -- all three steps of the brief and every part of them, marked built, half built or still to come -- and the page simply prints what the engine says, so the two cannot drift apart. Each part that depends on an outside service says so in its own line, and because the page asks the engine which services are actually switched on, the day a key arrives those lines change by themselves. Underneath sit the three outside services with what each one unlocks, what it costs and the name of its setting: the AI reader, which is on and has cost fifty-two cents so far; address checking and search, which are both still waiting for credits. As it stands the page reports twelve of twenty-three parts built, four half built, seven to come, and five finished parts sitting idle for want of a key. The last job of the day was the emails themselves, which the user called junk. They were right, and for a reason nobody had noticed: not one of them was signed. Each ended on its question with no name, no company, no reply address and no way to tell us to stop, which is how a good sentence still lands in a spam folder, and in the United States it is also not allowed for a commercial email. Every email now ends with who it is from, Legal Soft and the site, the address replies go to, a postal address and a plain line about how to stop; whatever has not been filled in is left blank rather than invented, and the screen says in red that these are not ready to send until it is. The words were rewritten too, from what Legal Soft actually offers rather than generalities: people who are already vetted, interviews that cost nothing, someone able to start in under three days. Each email now asks for something small and specific, two candidate profiles, instead of asking a busy partner for a call. What did not change is the thinking behind the sequence: the first line is still the firm's own need, the offer is still extra hands rather than replacing anyone, and the third email still argues in the firm's own fee terms. Read back, that first pass was still too clipped to send: no greeting, a lowercase fragment for a subject line, and an ending that stopped dead after the question. So the whole set was written again properly. Each subject now says something the firm already knows about itself, such as the role it has had open since August or the two seats it is advertising at once, because that is what makes someone open it. Each email opens with a greeting, runs three or four short paragraphs, asks for one small thing, and closes with a sign-off above the signature. The rule book was changed to match, since it had told us to write short lowercase notes, and the tests that guard the wording were re-pinned line by line. One thing that came out of it is worth knowing: the saved text is wrapped to a fixed width, but a firm's name and a role and a date all change length when they are filled in, so the lines of a sent email can never match the lines of the file. Letting each paragraph flow when it is written is the answer, and that is still a decision waiting on the user.

## 19 September 2026

While the credits for address checking and for search are being arranged, the user asked for the last stage of the brief to be worked on: the emails a lead is sent over its whole lifecycle, day by day, on a screen of its own. The engine already wrote nine emails for every lead from written templates, spread over four weeks, but they only showed at the bottom of each lead's page and nobody could change a word of them. There is now an Email journey screen under Handover. It opens on a line of days with a square for each email on the day it goes, the first on Day 1 and the last on Day 29, and below it every email as it would actually be sent, written by the engine itself for an invented example firm (its kind of practice, the need the engine found, whether its case system is known, and whether it is on the full track or the slower one can all be chosen) or for any real lead, opened from that lead's page. Each email says which version of it the firm gets and why, and the other versions sit beneath it. Any wording can be changed for every lead at once, with the details it may fill in offered as buttons, and the engine refuses what the brief's rules forbid: the words that read as replacing someone's staff, a detail some firms lack (which would drop the lead rather than send an email with a gap), or losing the paragraph each kind of firm gets. The days can be moved too, as long as each email comes after the one before. The original wording and days are always kept and one click brings them back, and every place an email appears (a lead's page, the Actual leads checks, the home page and the vendor's sheet) reads the same saved wording, so none of them can disagree. Building it showed that the days had been written into the engine twice, so changing them in its settings did nothing; they now come from one place. Two things were found and left for later, both written down: every email keeps the hard line breaks of the file it was typed in, which looks ragged once pasted into a mail program on a phone (a small change, waiting on the user's word because it changes the look of every email), and the Day 26 email counts how long a job post has been open as of the day it was drafted rather than the day it goes out, which belongs with saving each lead's journey when it is handed to a vendor. The side of the screen says who gets which emails, what to do when a firm replies, says not now or opts out, and the rules every email keeps. A lead's page now counts its emails from Day 1, calls them emails, and links to its journey. Every engine test and every screen test passes, and the screen was photographed at desk and phone width, including the editor refusing a banned word as it was typed.

## 18 September 2026

The day opened with a review instead of a feature: two independent read-only inspections of the code, one hunting bugs in everything changed the day before, one tracing the whole path a raw lead takes from a sheet row to a qualified lead. The bug hunt found four things, and the worst was self-inflicted the previous evening: a patch had slipped a helper function between a label and the function it was meant to label, so the engine's own registry pointed the "search for people's LinkedIn profiles" step at the wrong function — every research job and the nightly run would have failed at that step, and no existing test could see it because they all called the function directly rather than through the registry. It is fixed, and a new test now checks that every step the engine's flows and jobs name really is registered as a runnable step, so that mistake cannot happen quietly again. The other three: an address read off a site carried its printed text along as an extra field, so two sources describing the same office never counted as agreeing; the qualified-leads screen was doing every lead's homework on every page load instead of letting the database narrow the list first; and bulk decisions in the work queue could not qualify, reopen or hand off a lead even though the single-lead buttons could. All four fixed, tested, committed. Later the whole thing was brought up on the local machine and checked end to end: the database, the engine on port 8000 and the screens on port 3100 all answer, and the screens reach the engine through their own address, though with no firms loaded yet every list shows its empty state. In the evening the user set out, by hand, how the engine should work in three steps — sort the raw leads, enrich them in batches, hand them over — and asked for a cross-check before any code. The brief: a sheet carries five things (firm name, website, location, job post, firm size); a row with all five is Batch 1, a row with no firm name is thrown away, a row with a name but gaps is Batch 2; batches are cut into groups of 250 and Batch 1 is always worked first; Batch 2 firms are first identified by a search; an AI, not a scraper, writes the pain analysis and an enrichment report; finished leads land in a pool called Actual Leads, and a handover cycle writes a document for a vendor and marks those leads as handed over. Nothing was built; the whole engine was read against that brief and the result written up as a new plan. Most of the individual finders already exist — people, addresses, LinkedIn profiles, the careers page, the nine-touch drafts, the lifecycle with its outcome buttons. What is missing is everything that ties them into the engine described: no row is ever judged or discarded (a row with no firm name quietly becomes a firm called "Unknown Firm"), there is no Batch 1 or Batch 2, no queue and no priority, enrichment never starts by itself, the website finder Batch 2 depends on is built but reachable only from the command line, the job post and firm size a sheet carries are stored and then read by nothing, pain is three fixed rules with no AI involved, there is no enrichment report, and there is no vendor, no handover document and no record of who was given which lead. Two faults turned up on the way and are first in the plan: the Research button on the screen runs a shorter list of steps than the full run — it skips the AI's reading of the site, the check of any address found on the site, and the step that moves a lead along, so a lead researched from the screen says "researching" forever however much was found — and the sheet of ready leads meant for the vendor skips the rule that keeps paying customers off it. And two keys stand in front of everything: without the address-checking key the email predictor skips every person, and without the search key Batch 2's search is blocked after a few hundred firms. The user settled four of the six open questions the same evening. A sheet missing a whole column still loads, with every row in Batch 2. Both batches are cut into groups of 250. The crawl goes down to five pages, with the phone and address taken from the business listing instead of the contact page, and the loss measured. And every lead that finishes enrichment lands in Actual Leads showing what it still lacks, though only a finished one can be handed to a vendor. Still open: whether the size a sheet gives should count as the firm's size, and the two keys. Then building started, with the mending the plan puts first. The Research button no longer keeps its own list of steps beside the full run's: both read one recipe, so a lead researched from the screen now gets the AI's reading of its site, has its addresses checked, and moves forward through its stages instead of saying "researching" for good. Wiring that in exposed a trap that would have been expensive: the step that sends an uncertain firm to the more powerful and pricier AI treated "research this one firm" as "escalate it no matter what", so every click would have paid for the expensive model; now it escalates only a firm the cheaper reading was unsure about. The sheet of ready leads meant for a vendor now obeys the same rules as every other list, so a paying customer, a firm on the do-not-contact list, a parked lead or a competitor's customer can no longer reach it, and the Qualified screen says how many leads those rules held back and why. The new tests caught one more fault, left by the morning's own fix: a lead whose decision-maker had a name but no address at all was quietly disappearing from the Qualified screen instead of showing as one step away. The lights that say which paid services are switched on now ask the engine rather than a setting nothing uses, and today they honestly read: AI on, address checking off, business-listing lookup off. Over twelve hundred engine tests and the screens' own checks pass, and the database, the engine and the screens were brought back up detached from this session, so they keep running after it. In the evening the user handed over the look of the whole platform, and every screen was redesigned. Each screen was photographed with sample data first, a separate review looked at how people use them, and the new design was rebuilt screen by screen with a fresh photograph after each change. The idea comes from how court papers are bound, in a blue cover with the document on white inside: the background and the side menu are now a pale legal blue, and each screen is one white sheet laid on it. The side menu follows the user's own three steps (raw leads, enrichment, handover), and the screen you are on shows as a tab joined to the sheet. A firm's name is set large in a legal typeface, the way a case is titled, and the sentence that proves a firm's need is still the only thing marked in yellow. The home page now opens on the strongest proof the engine has found, with who to write to and the first email ready to copy, then shows where the three steps stand, and only then the guide and the questions the user asked for. A lead's page became two columns, reading on the left and working on the right, cutting it to less than half its old length. The work queue lost most of its clutter, the upload screen explains what happens to a sheet before there is one, and "Lists" and "Qualified leads" are now "Sheets" and "Actual leads", the words of the user's own brief. Two small faults turned up and were fixed: the screens never finished loading when opened at the machine's number address instead of its name, and the message shown when the engine is down gave a start command this machine blocks. The separate review of how people use the screens then landed, agreed with most of what had been done, and added a few things that were put in straight away: the screens now say plainly when a missing key is holding everything up ("Address checking is off, so no lead can be ready to hand over", and which key to add), the blue button on a lead is always the one thing that lead needs next, the steps of a research job speak in words instead of engine counters, the upload screen shows how many rows carry each of the five fields the brief asks for, and two different kinds of "ready" no longer share one name. Last, the user asked for the screens on port 5000 and the engine on a different port, without disturbing another project's website that runs on port 3000 on the same machine. The screens now open at localhost:5000 and the engine answers on port 8100; the old servers were stopped one by one after checking each was ours, and the other project's server kept running throughout. Two things were tightened on the way. The engine had been set to trust pages from port 3000, the port first planned for these screens, which on this machine meant the other project's pages could have read our contacts; it now trusts only our own screens, and a test holds it to that. And the screens' server had been listening to the whole local network, not just this computer, so anyone on the same Wi-Fi could have read the engine's data through it; it now answers this computer only, which is what the engine itself always did. Everything was checked after the move: the engine, the screens reaching it, every screen loading fully under both of the machine's own addresses, and the tests. The screens are empty for a plain reason, not a fault: the live database was set up the night before and nothing has been loaded into it yet, so every list waits for the first sheet.

## 17 September 2026

We stopped and re-planned instead of building. Looking at the whole project from the top, most of the work is getting the data — reading a firm's website properly, searching the web for what the site doesn't say, and finding each person's LinkedIn profile — and the build so far had gone deep on the later steps (tiering, email drafts, screens) while those three stayed weak. The new plan puts them first, in order, each measured on the 242 test firms before it runs over all 22,000, and pushes the outreach writing to the end where it belongs. Three things were pinned down along the way: the sheet reader must never require a column — the AI reads whatever is uploaded and research fills the gaps; DuckDuckGo has to be replaced with a paid search engine because it blocks us after a few hundred queries (Serper recommended, about fifty dollars for the whole list); and the screens need to become something a team can log into and use, not a one-person project. The morning also had a self-inflicted outage — Postgres had been started inside a task that was later stopped, which took the database down and made every page return an error — fixed by starting the database and the engine detached from anything that can be stopped by accident. Then the first chunk of the new plan was built: uploading a sheet no longer assumes anything about its columns. The AI reads the header row and a dozen sample rows once, says which column is which and why, and its answer is checked against the actual values before it is trusted — a column it calls "email" whose cells are web addresses is overruled. The only things a sheet must have are a firm name and some way to reach the firm (a website, an email, or a location); size, practice, people and the rest are marked "filled by research" instead of "missing". The loader also stops reading whole sheets into memory: a 100,000-row file was read in under a second at 25 MB, in batches of 500, so the size of a sheet no longer matters. Every outside provider — the AI, the search engine, the email finder and verifier — is now chosen by one setting each, so switching Serper for Brave or Hunter for NeverBounce is a line in a config file, not a code change. The second chunk followed: the crawler now reads six kinds of page per firm — home, about, contact, the attorneys and team pages, testimonials, careers — instead of twelve kinds and thirty pages, and the list lives in one config file that every part of the engine asks, so adding a kind back is one line. On the 232 test firms that cut the pages read by nearly half while losing almost nothing: the number of firms whose size we know went up by half, because the attorneys a firm names on its own team page now count as its size (one firm was correctly ruled out for listing 51), practice areas and people found were unchanged, and five firms lost their fee-model reading because their "no fee unless we win" line only appeared on a practice page. Doing this uncovered a quiet bug worth more than the chunk itself: a fact read off a page fetched later — say a news page — kept counting as the freshest thing known about a firm even after the engine stopped reading that kind of page, so old readings could outlive their source. Now each reading pass replaces everything that pass said before about a firm, and 617 such leftover facts were cleared from the test firms. The third chunk put the web search behind one door: the engine that answers is chosen by which key is in the settings — Serper (Google's results) first, Brave as the fallback, DuckDuckGo only when there is no key — and every result, every claim and every logged query says which engine produced it. When the engine can look up a business listing directly, the Google Business Profile (rating, review count, phone, the listing itself) is read from that instead of guessed from snippets, and every search for a person now includes the firm's city so namesakes in other towns stop matching. It is built and tested against recorded answers; the first real run waits for the Serper key. The fourth chunk did the same for people's email addresses: Hunter is wired in as the service that finds a decision-maker's address from their name and the firm's domain, or finds the decision-makers themselves when the site named nobody, and as the service that checks whether an address is real — with NeverBounce kept as a one-line switch for the checking part. Hunter's own verdicts are translated onto our stricter scale (a "catch-all" domain counts as a failure, never a pass), a person is never paid for twice, and nothing runs until the Hunter key is in place. The day ended with a new screen, "Qualified leads": the one place that shows only the leads on which every step is done — site read, firm fits, decision-maker named, address on file and verified, LinkedIn profile found, journey drafted — with a second tab for the ones one step away and a line saying what is holding them back, plus a one-click sheet of the ready ones for the sending vendor. Today it is empty, honestly so: nothing in the store has a verified address yet, and that is exactly the work the two keys unlock. Last thing: at the user's request the whole store was emptied — the purchased list, the test crawl and everything derived from them — so the platform starts clean and testing begins from a sheet the user uploads themselves. A compressed copy of what was removed sits in an ignored backup folder in case it is ever wanted; the platform itself has nothing in it but its own rules. And the home page became the manual: a six-step walkthrough from uploading a sheet to exporting a ready lead, a line on what each tab is for, and ten plain-words questions and answers — how to get in, what a sheet needs, how long a thousand leads take (about two hours with the search and address keys, most of it the engine reading sites in the background; the better part of a day without them), which keys unlock what, what "ready" means, and what the platform will never do (send email, scrape LinkedIn or job boards). That first version read like a document and was sent back as tasteless, so the page got one bold thing: a lead that builds itself in front of you — an invented firm going from a row on a sheet to a verified website, the people and the highlighted sentence that proves the need, the tier, every check green, and the first email's subject line — playing once when the page opens, and then serving as the instrument of the walkthrough, where clicking a step shows exactly what that step leaves behind. The questions became an index with the answer beside it. One correction to the day's plan came from the user: Hunter, which had been wired in to find and verify addresses, sells a lead-finding product of its own, and this is an in-house engine — so finding addresses stays our own scraping (the site, search results, the firm's address pattern, which already covered most named people on the test crawl), and only the one thing scraping cannot do, checking that an address really exists, is rented from a plain verification service. The Hunter connection stays in the code, switched off. And the obvious thing got built instead: when a firm has no known address at all, the engine now writes out the likely addresses for a decision-maker from their first name, last name and the firm's domain — john.smith, john, jsmith and so on, most common first — and has the verification service check each until one comes back real; the hit becomes the person's address and teaches the engine that firm's pattern for everyone else there. A firm whose mail server accepts anything is spotted on the first answer and costs nothing more. About a cent per try, two or three tries on average.

## 16 September 2026

The "research this firm" buttons on the screen looked like they were working but nothing ever happened — the job was being handed to a worker before it had been saved, so the worker went looking for it, found nothing, and gave up silently. Jobs are now saved first and then run on their own thread, and anything left hanging from a crashed run is marked failed when the engine starts, so the screen never claims work is in progress when it isn't. We also changed how we look people up: instead of one search per person, we now run one search per firm and match every profile on the results page against the people we already know about, which takes the full list from roughly 250,000 searches down to about 30,000 — the difference between a run that gets us blocked and one that finishes. Finally the project was split into three repositories that all live inside the one Lead Gen Engine folder — the engine itself at the top, brain underneath it (the plans, the decisions and this report) and frontend beside it (the screens) — and the loose PDFs, superseded drafts and old run logs were cleared out of the code folder, so it is now obvious where anything lives without anything leaving the project folder.
