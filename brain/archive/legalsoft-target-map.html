Ideal Customer Profile & Lead Collection Strategy

Legal Soft Target Map

Who to target, and how to collect them. The profile is built from how law firms actually make money — not from anybody's marketing claims — so it holds up whether or not the numbers on a website are true.

§1

The need model

Start from the buyer, not the product. A law firm needs outsourced legal staff when four conditions hold together. Miss any one and the firm either does not need it, or needs it but will not buy.

Every filter in this document is derived from one of these four. That is deliberate — a targeting rule that cannot be traced back to a reason the firm would buy is a rule that will quietly select the wrong firms.

Condition 1
Delegable volume

The firm generates repetitive, procedural work in quantity — records requests, form preparation, status calls, document collection.

Bespoke, judgment-heavy work at low volume cannot be handed to anyone, at any price.

→ practice area §2
Condition 2
Remote-executable

The work needs a phone, a browser and the firm's case system — not a courtroom, a notary stamp, or a physical file room.

This is a property of the task, which is why the wedge is a role, not a practice.

→ role selection §5
Condition 3
Cost-centre work

The work is not resold to a client at a markup. Nobody is billed for the hour, so a cheaper hour is margin rather than lost revenue.

This is the condition that most targeting misses entirely.

→ fee model §3
Condition 4
Local hiring blocked

The firm cannot hire the person locally — too slow, too expensive, or no qualified applicants at all.

Without this, the firm just hires. This condition is also the most observable from outside.

→ size §4 · signals §9
Why this ordering matters for collection

Conditions 1–3 are stable properties of a firm. They change rarely, so they can be determined once and stored — this is the universe problem, solved by breadth.

Condition 4 is a transient state. A firm is blocked on hiring for a few weeks, then it is not. This is the in-market problem, solved by freshness. Building one system for both is the most common way these projects fail.

The pattern underneath

Every practice at the top of this table shares one trait: the firm must gather documents from third parties who are slow. Medical providers, employers, manufacturers, government agencies. That chasing is high-volume, low-judgment, entirely remote, and it directly delays the firm getting paid.

That single characteristic is a better predictor of need than the practice label, and it is why PI, mass tort, immigration and SSDI cluster at the top despite being unrelated areas of law.

§3

Fee model — the axis most targeting misses

Two firms can look identical — same practice, same size, same city — and have opposite economics on this purchase, because of how they bill.

In a firm that bills by the hour, a paralegal is inventory. The firm pays them a wage and resells their time to the client at a multiple. That person is a profit centre. Replacing their hours with cheaper hours that cannot be resold at the same rate removes revenue.

In a firm that works on contingency or a flat fee, the same paralegal is pure overhead. Nobody is billed for their time. The fee is fixed at the outcome or agreed up front, so every hour of support labour is subtracted from the firm's own margin. Move that hour to a cheaper resource and the difference goes straight to the partners.

Same product, same role, opposite arithmetic. This is condition 3, and it is the strongest single filter in the profile.

Fee model → what support staff represents → what can be sold
Fee modelSupport staff isSellableHow to detect it
ContingencyPI, mass tort, some employment Overhead carried for months against a fee that arrives at settlement. EverythingLead with roles that accelerate the fee — demand writing, lien negotiation — not roles that merely reduce cost. "no fee unless we win", "no recovery no fee", "contingency fee"
Fee-shiftingemployment, lemon law, consumer, some civil rights Overhead. The losing party pays fees, so the firm's own labour is still an unbilled input. EverythingEconomically identical to contingency and far less contested by competitors. "fees paid by the defendant / manufacturer / employer"
Flat feeimmigration, bankruptcy, criminal, estate A direct subtraction from a fee already fixed. Every extra hour on a matter reduces the margin on that specific matter. Keep hourly firms in the database

They are the weakest fit, not a zero. Excluding them at collection time makes this whole axis impossible to verify — they are never contacted, so evidence that contradicts it can never arrive. Collect them, classify them, and put a deliberate slice into outreach on a non-billable role so the assumption gets tested against real outcomes rather than repeated.

Attorney count says how big a firm is, not how overloaded it is. Three cheap, observable attributes predict need better, and all three are collectible from sources we are already touching.

Geography

No demand weighting in version one. Weighting states because that is where a company has sold before is circular — it may simply reflect where the founders' network is, and it can point at a saturated market rather than an open one.

Prioritise collection by density instead: metropolitan areas first, because more firms per unit of collection effort. That is an efficiency decision, not a demand assumption. Let close-rate data name the good geographies later.

The ICP

Everything above, resolved into tiers. Fee model gates first; practice area refines. Each tier carries the role that should open the conversation.

1B Immigrationflat fee · bilingual need Fixed fee per matter against enormous form and evidence volume, plus a client base that frequently needs Spanish. Every hour saved on a matter is margin on that matter. Bilingual legal assistant

4 Hourly practicesbusiness, real estate, tax, IP Support staff is billable, so only genuinely unbillable functions are sellable. Narrow but not zero — and the segment that tests whether the fee-model axis is real. Bookkeeperor marketing assistant
Open with the role that touches no client

Roles that speak to clients — intake, case management — carry accent concerns, trust concerns and professional-responsibility anxiety. They are a hard first sale into a firm that has never outsourced.

Roles that touch no client — document collection, demand writing, lien negotiation, bookkeeping — carry almost none of that. Open there. Client-facing roles become easy once the firm has watched the arrangement work for a quarter, and that expansion is the firm's own idea rather than ours.

§8

The triangulation

Three discovery sources produce a firm candidate; the firm's own site then qualifies it. No source is skippable — drop the bar directory and you lose the partner's name, drop business listings and you lose the website, drop the site crawl and you lose every attribute the tier depends on.

The bar directory is the differentiated move

Generic B2B contact databases are thin on small law firms — they cover companies with LinkedIn presence and marketing departments, which most 4-attorney firms are not. Bar directories are public records that name every practising attorney and tie them to a firm. That is how the decision-maker gets found for firms no commercial database covers properly, and almost nobody doing outbound into this market bothers with it.

§9

In-market signals

The universe tells us who could buy. Signals tell us who is trying to solve this problem right now — condition 4, observable from outside.

Strategy, not just ranking

Signal 1 is the strongest evidence and also the one every competing vendor sees the same morning. A firm that posts a paralegal role gets pitched by several of them that week. Treating it as the whole strategy makes speed the only advantage, and speed is trivially matched.

So run signal 1 at volume, but win it on the opening line — the specific role, the date it was posted, their actual case system, the partner's real name from the bar record. And put disproportionate effort into signals 3, 6 and 8, which are weaker evidence but effectively uncontested.

§10

Getting to the decision-maker

In a firm of this size there is no committee. There is one person who signs, one who feels the pain, and one who can quietly kill it.

RoleWhoWhy they matterFindable via
Signs Owner or managing partner — usually the name on the door Decides alone, in days. Motivated by taking home more and reclaiming time, not by cost-per-hour tables. bar directory + team page
Champions Firm administrator, office manager, director of operations — appears above roughly eight staff The single best first contact where one exists. They are the person who posted the job nobody applied to. They forward a relevant email the same day. team page — not in bar records, they are not attorneys
Blocks Senior in-house paralegal, or the partner's own risk instinct Fears replacement, or fears being the partner who put client information somewhere it should not be. Often reads the owner's inbox. team page
Two things this dictates about collection

Capture both people, not one. Owner and administrator. A record with only a generic inbox is not a lead, and a record with only the owner misses the person most likely to act.

Frame as capacity, never replacement — from the very first line. The blocker is frequently the person who opens the mail, and a message that reads as "replace your staff" dies in that inbox without ever reaching the owner.

§11

Collection sequence

Do not harvest the whole country and filter afterwards. Collect one segment completely, prove the chain end to end on a set small enough to check by hand, then widen.

The alternative — harvest broadly, then discard most of it — spends the majority of the collection effort on firms that were never going to qualify, and hides errors until they are expensive.

Phased collection · each phase proves something before the next widens
PhaseCollectProvesMove on when
0 — Probe Three metros, tier 1A only. A few hundred firms, fully processed through every step. That the whole chain works, and what each stage's real yield is. Every number in a plan like this is an estimate until this phase replaces it. A hand-checked sample of 50 records is accurate on every attribute.
1 — Beachhead Tier 1A nationally — PI and mass tort, 2–25 attorneys, metros first by density. Coverage at scale, and that identity resolution holds when the same firm appears in three sources across fifty states. Duplicate check is clean and decision-maker coverage holds.
2 — Signals No new firms. Add the signal layer over the beachhead. That in-market detection actually fires, and how many firms light up in a given month. Signals fire at a stable weekly rate and reply data starts distinguishing them.
3 — Adjacent Tier 1B immigration, then tier 2 fee-shifting. That the profile generalises beyond PI — the first real test of the fee-model axis. Tier 2 converts within range of tier 1A.
4 — Breadth & holdouts Tier 3, plus a deliberate slice of tier 4 hourly firms on a non-billable role. Whether the fee-model axis is a fact or a good story. This is the only phase that can disprove it. —
Resolve identity before crawling, not after

The same firm arrives from three sources under three names. Deduplicate before the site crawl, or the most expensive stage in collection runs three times on the same firm — and the same partner later receives three emails from three addresses, which ends the programme faster than any technical failure.

Grade B is a product, not a reject pile

A meaningful share of small firms will yield a named partner, a working phone and a live signal — but no verifiable personal email address. Those records are not failures. They are a phone-and-research list, worked by a different motion.

Deleting them throws away a large slice of the collection effort. Emailing them anyway, on a guessed address, damages the ability to email anyone.

§13

Keeping the list alive

A collected list is a depreciating asset. People leave firms, firms merge and rebrand, sites are rebuilt, roles get filled. Collection is not a project that finishes — the maintenance loop is the product.

LoopCadenceWhat it does
Signal sweepNightlyCheck every qualified firm for open roles. This is the loop that turns a static list into a pipeline, and the one where lateness costs the most — a role found on day two converts far better than the same role found on day twenty.
Review sweepWeeklyTier 1A and 1B only. New reviews matched against the intake-failure phrase set.
Advertising & tort sweepMonthlyTier 1A. Detects volume spikes and new mass tort campaigns.
Freshness sweepRolling, whole list every 90 daysRe-check the site. If it changed, re-read the attributes. If a contact no longer appears on the team page, re-resolve the person.
Re-poolingContinuousA firm that did not respond is not spent. It returns to the pool with its verified contact intact and waits for the next signal. Most firms are not in market today; the point of holding a current record is being ready on the day they are.
What decides whether any of this was right

Every closed deal should be traceable to the signal that surfaced it. After a quarter, that one join — outcome by signal type, and by tier — is what tells you which signals to invest in and whether the fee-model axis is real.

The rankings in this document are reasoned, not measured. They are a starting hypothesis, and the only thing that makes them correct is replacing them with what the outcomes say.

Scope: ideal customer profile and lead collection strategy. Implementation detail — schema, components, orchestration — is in the Pipeline Build Spec.
Tier rankings, signal ordering and proxy choices are reasoned from how law firms are structured and paid, and are to be replaced by outcome data as it arrives. Two data sources are deliberately excluded: direct job-board scraping and automated professional-network extraction.
